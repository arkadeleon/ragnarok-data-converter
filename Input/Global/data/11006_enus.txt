Publisher’s Note:
At present, it is impossible to verify the precise origin or place of transmission of this tale. Some claim it was found in a traveler’s bag; others say it has long been passed among those called bards as a bittersweet song of love. Yet the story features warriors who fight in place of the goddess Freyja and the enemy nation’s Odin, and near the end, the heroine is depicted as being “chosen by the heavens,” much like how our current Pope is chosen by the Goddess. These elements make it clear that the work touches upon religious matters—highly sensitive to us—and that cannot simply be overlooked.

That said, based on the setting and events described, the most common view is that this is not a record of real history, but a fictional work composed to emphasize a particular aspect of Lady Freyja, the goddess of love and beauty. For a long time, the tale remained hidden in the shadows and never surfaced publicly, dismissed as irreverent for using the divine in the context of romantic relationships. Still, it seemed too precious to bury, and thus we have compiled it anew.

With no known title and no proper original manuscript to be found, restoring the story has been difficult—but if you, the readers, enjoy it, that will be enough for us.
– Editorial Department

The sky was choked with dark clouds, as though it might pour rain at any moment. The land—once surely blue beneath a clear sky—was now blackened and scorched, spewing acrid smoke upward. Everywhere you looked, a mournful air. Wherever you went, a bleak earth and a bleak heaven. Through a gray fog, the hurried shadows of people flickered into view.

They were locked in fierce battle.

They leapt over and trampled half-burned ashes and the cold bodies of enemies—or their own comrades—while the clamor of spears and blades rang out in chaos.

A brutal clash between those who served the Goddess and those who did not. Those who crossed to this land by the Goddess’s will and built a new home, and those who had lived here for generations—perhaps such bitter antagonism had been inevitable. The tribes native to this land welcomed the foreigners warmly at first. But peace did not last long. Among the natives who had lost the initiative, an anti-Freyja faction began to rise, and in the end, those whose beliefs could not align chose even war.

Once friends. Once neighbors.
Now a savage fight—raising blades against one another in the name of faith.

Across the blackened grassland, a vivid crimson line was drawn. Like a living serpent, it stretched long over the dark plain, as if marking this place as a battlefield, painting red as it went. Sticky crimson lines. People falling atop them. The red line hesitated for a moment, then merged with fresh crimson seeping from the cooling bodies, becoming thicker—slower—oozing down the slope.

The sky, as if determined to witness the final confrontation, kept filling itself with more and more storm clouds, sometimes letting out an indistinct, rumbling cry.

“Is it… going to rain…?”

The young man steadied his ragged breathing and adjusted his grip on his sword.
How many days has it been, fighting? When does this hateful banquet of blood end?

Leaning against an overturned wagon, he looked up at the sky. It was brutally black. It felt like rain. Ah… rain…

^FA8072Clinging to the window railing, Kras held out a freshly picked, vivid red rose to the girl he called “Sen.” But the only response he received was a single sulky line from the pouting girl.
“But it’s too late. Look at the sky!”
At Sen’s complaint, Kras reluctantly looked up.
Ah… heavens. Why would you clear up like a lie…?
Was the rain you sprinkled this morning not rain at all, but only dew?
Kras could do nothing but curse the bone-dry sky and beg Sen, now turned sullen and sharp. This was already the fifth time. Why did Sen insist on wanting a rose that bloomed on a rainy day? In a region where even seeing a rose was difficult—where you were lucky if the sand-wind didn’t blow—was a rain-blooming rose really that common? Besides, the south-central part of the continent where Kras and Sen lived was highland and semi-desert; water was scarce. A cactus might endure, but for a flower—especially a rose—to spread its petals alone was a climate too cruel. And yet his beloved demanded a rose that bloomed on a rainy day even under such harsh conditions.
Because of that, he had poured precious water—water they could barely afford to spare—into trivial rose cultivation, earning cold stares and mockery both at home and in the village. Still blaming the sky, Kras recalled how, two years ago, he had impulsively made that promise to her.^000000

Clink. Clank. Clink.
The young man flinched at the sound of armor from somewhere nearby. Who is it? Cold sweat ran down his back. Enemy soldiers? No… it can’t be.

He forced himself to calm down and tightened the dagger strapped to his left wrist. It was Kras’s habit—insurance in case he couldn’t use his hands, or lost his sword. And so the other wouldn’t notice, he quietly, carefully took hold of his sword with his right hand.

The clinking of armor drew closer, reaching the back of the wagon he was using as cover. Very close. Should I strike first…?

He rose slightly, low to the ground. His mind was chaos—ten thousand thoughts at once. If they knew where I was and came to kill me? Or… what if they came to tell me we’d won? What if they smiled and said, “You don’t have to hide anymore”?

The thought reached that far, and he shook his head hard as if to deny it.

As if that could be true.

This endless melee already looked to him like nothing more than a slaughterhouse of soldiers who had lost their reason to long fighting. They all screamed the names of their gods—but what “battle for the will of the divine” was this? It was only murder committed under borrowed holy names.

And yet—he couldn’t help a bitter smile, knowing he too had driven steel into others just to survive.

Still crouched low, Kras slid into the blind spot created by a wide board broken off the wagon. The armored footsteps stopped. Kras stopped as well, holding his breath.

A big one.
The hulking man, seemingly unaware that Kras was nearby and targeting him, cautiously scanned the area—then tried to slip quickly into a pit gouged into the earth by the crashed wagon.

At the same instant, Kras lunged.

His left arm hooked under the man’s throat, pinning him, and the dagger strapped to that arm bared its sharp edge, pressing up against the man’s neck. The giant flinched, but Kras tightened the hold from behind and spoke fast, low.

“Which side are you with?”

“Like I’d tell you.”

Kras’s dagger nicked the man’s neck. Red blood ran down along the blade.

“Which side?”

“Why don’t you kill me?”

Kras fell silent.

Why don’t you kill him?
It would be over if he simply cut.

That question had been haunting him since the moment he put steel to the man’s throat. Ha—he almost laughed. After killing and being killed like this, was there any guarantee he’d live? At last, he spoke aloud what he had only repeated inside.

“Why… do I have to kill…?”

“Hah!”

The giant snorted loudly. Holding someone’s life in your hand and saying only “Why do I have to kill?”—absurd.

The giant shrugged.

“You seem like the kind of fellow I might actually talk to. I don’t know what answer you want, but it’s a war that began in the name of the gods. In wars like this, the side that ends up with the most heads still attached wins. Isn’t that right?”

He paused and took a deep breath. Kras couldn’t see his face, but the bobbing of the man’s head told him he was laughing under his breath—until the blade digging into his throat stopped it, and he let out a heavy sigh.

“Go on,”

At Kras’s low voice, the giant shrugged again and continued. He nudged the dagger’s edge aside with a finger, but Kras didn’t stop him—only waited.

“And… on a battlefield, there’s something we must be able to tell apart. Allied uniforms and enemy uniforms. No matter what, you’ve got to distinguish those two, or you’re not a proper soldier. Right… Kras?”

Kras froze. Who was this—someone who knew his name?

He began to examine the man’s outfit. A priest’s armor thrown on carelessly—no matter how you looked at it, he seemed like a mercenary. Of course you can’t tell—

…Wait.

Kras’s eyes stopped at the man’s waist.

A wide reddish-brown sash tied haphazardly, and a small holy relic hanging from it. Even scorched and splattered with blood, it was unmistakably a symbol of the Goddess.

Then who… was he, to know Kras?

“And… how’s that young lady these days?”

“What?”

The giant twisted his body toward him. No… no way…

The moment Kras recognized his face, he threw his arms around him.

“What have I… what have I done…? Beren… Beren?!”

Beren soothed Kras, who clung to him and kept begging forgiveness, collapsing in relief. Then Beren looked at Kras for a moment—Kras standing there hollow-eyed, disgusted with himself for raising a blade against a friend—before he silently began to deepen the pit using wagon debris.

It didn’t take long. Beren only smoothed the edges and covered it with the broken wagon, but it would serve as an excellent hiding place.

By then, Kras had calmed somewhat. Fidgeting with the dagger blade he had aimed at Beren, he looked at him. When Beren sat beside him, Kras finally spoke.

“How did you know it was me?”

Beren laughed low.

“Among the people I know, I think you’re the only one who carries a weapon on your left arm instead of a shield. Guess I got lucky. A reunion after years… and what a ridiculous situation.”

“Yeah… it is.”

The sky grew even darker, casting heavier shadows. It was so dark you couldn’t even tell whether it was day or night. The clangor of spears, swords, and armor that had been so loud had begun to fade. Now and then, a dying scream rose from far away, but…

As the battle neared its end, the sky too seemed to be losing patience—thunder and lightning cracking down again and again.

“Feels like it’s going to rain.”

Kras muttered. Beren looked up and nodded.

“Seems the fighting’s almost over.”
“Just like that. After all that ferocity…”
“Kras. You’re lucky. You didn’t get swept into the center of it.”
“I was in the third line.”
“And?”
Beren’s question made Kras stare at him briefly, then return his gaze to the sky.

“You really don’t know? I was the one who set the fires while pulling back along the perimeter. Yesterday, the grassland was gilded gold under autumn sunlight—now it’s… it’s like this. It was ‘necessary’ for the operation, but…”

Beren nodded.

“Ah… well. Either way, you and I are lucky. We didn’t get swallowed by those madmen who live for killing. Look at that hill—those idiots were already turning it into a dogfight. What are they trying to accomplish, fighting among the survivors? Lunatics.”

Tap.

A raindrop landed on Kras’s cheek. A flash—lightning—then, with a rolling rumble, thick rain poured down in a roar.

As the drops struck the brittle earth, ash sprang up. The droplets that bounced back from the ground were a deep crimson. Watching the red water hopping across the soil in silence, Beren suddenly spoke.

“By the way—what about that young lady? The one… Sen? Sen what was it? Your girlfriend who asked you for that strange wish.”

“Senia.”

“Right, Miss Senia! But how did you end up making such a ridiculous promise?”

At Beren’s sudden question, Kras briefly wondered if it was even right to talk like this on a battlefield—then what did it matter? A fight that had already lost its purpose.

Maybe, as Beren implied, it was better to think of her instead.

Her—white as a lily.
