^8B008BThe Great Sage “Varmunt” (A.W. 667–???)^000000

The Great Sage Varmunt was born in A.W. (After War) 667 in a small rural village of Schwarzwald.
Born as an only child to a diligent but impoverished farmer father and a gentle mother who worked as a housemaid, he began from an early age to display an intelligence and extraordinary talent unlike that of other children.

However, as his parents lacked the means to provide him with formal education, they introduced the child to Professor Heji, a former faculty member of Yuno National University who had retired to the countryside. Professor Heji recognized Varmunt’s exceptional nature at first sight and took him in as his disciple.

Later, at the age of fourteen, Varmunt entered Yuno National University as its youngest-ever student upon Professor Heji’s recommendation, receiving the finest education from the greatest professors of the era. While he excelled in every field, he especially distinguished himself in biology, archaeology, and ancient science.

Seven years later, the genius young man—only twenty-one—became the youngest professor in the history of Yuno National University. Although he did take the lectern as the youngest professor, he was far more absorbed in excavating and researching ancient ruins than in training future scholars.

Yet research into ancient ruins and ancient science—taboo within academic circles—presented him with many obstacles. Above all, the financial burden required for large-scale excavation projects was immense. Nevertheless, Company XXX (Note 1), which had long kept a close eye on Varmunt’s genius, promised him generous support, and Varmunt thereafter devoted himself even more fully to his studies.

Throughout his life, he immersed himself in the study of ancient ruins and ancient science, and Company XXX, drawing upon these achievements, underwent repeated leaps in growth and development.

Unlike most scholars, Varmunt did not pour his passion solely into academic inquiry; he also displayed outstanding genius and ability in literature, painting, and music. Devoting himself energetically to creative work, he left behind countless pieces until the day he died. Regrettably, despite his almost obsessive habit of organization, most of these works were not passed down to later generations, as his home was looted and burned during the “Mercenary Uprising” (Note 2).

Thus, though Varmunt left remarkable achievements in both science and the arts—admired in his own age and celebrated by posterity—he withdrew from the outside world in his later years, living in seclusion within his mansion, fortress-like in its scale and strength.

There, he sought to compile his personal research into a culmination and attempted to restore the ancient creative scientific technologies he had studied across his lifetime. After relentless efforts and experiments bordering on obsession, creations comparable to the ancient relics were ultimately born (Note 3), and he filled his mansion with these creations.

Varmunt’s end is not clear. In his final years, he effectively exiled himself, living surrounded by his creations, and no one witnessed either his death or his remains. The only clue is that his female disciple, ‘***’—the sole person who continued exchanging letters with him and thus served as his connection to the world—vanished without a trace at some point. Many suspect that this period coincided with Varmunt’s end.

For several years after he was presumed dead, his mansion and research facility remained abandoned. Then, during the Mercenary Uprising of A.W. 781, it was revealed to the world for the first time. Pursued by the suppression forces, the mercenaries made Varmunt’s mansion their final stronghold, occupying the great estate and holding out.

After roughly two months of standoff, the suppression forces finally launched an assault. The mercenaries resisted fiercely, but they were outnumbered and outmatched. In the brutal suppression—so extreme that not a single prisoner was left—most of Varmunt’s creations and the fruits of his extraordinary achievements were lost.

In the end, only the few surviving letters attributed to him, and the formulas and laws that anyone studying ancient science must learn, remain as proof of his existence and genius to this day.
